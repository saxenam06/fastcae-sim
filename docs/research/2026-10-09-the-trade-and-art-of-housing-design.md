## 1. In short

1. **Every housing in these studies is a designed object before any optimiser touches it.** It has a form (lobes round the gears, a round motor face, a cover), bosses, a split flange with ears, mounts, ports, and a first set of ribs placed by a designer along the load paths. The optimiser is then asked to *redistribute* a small mass (Ivanov: 749 g of ribs on a 7.7 kg housing; Kim: 0.2 % of the mass; Son 2020: 12 %) on that form. None of them optimised a blank shell, which is what our first loop did.
2. **The art has a vocabulary of about eight moves, visible in every figure:** the boss as a hub with spokes to a rim; a rib from each mount ear to the nearest bearing boss; ribs between bosses along the lines of centres; triangles, never open quadrilaterals; gussets at the root of a tall boss and under every flange ear; a continuous rim (the flange lip or a ring) that the ribs end on; bridges over ports; and walls kept thin between the ribs. Section 3 shows each in the figures, with its mechanics.
3. **The process is a loop of diagnosis, local design space, optimisation, reading by hand, and re-grade,** with the weak place found first by a measure that matters (bearing-centre displacement, local stiffness at points, strain energy of the mode that radiates, equivalent radiated power), and the design space drawn round that place. The optimiser's result is read into the vocabulary above, then sized by a small parameter study (rib width, then length and height). The gains reported are modest (tens of percent of a local measure) because the starting housings were already designed.
4. **Our housing's weakness is now measured, not guessed** (section 4): in the lobed housing the bearing seats turn *with* their end walls (75-95 % of each seat's rotation is the end wall's slope), the shafts' lines tilt because the lobes bend sideways (0.24-0.58 mrad), and the joint between the halves contributes nothing (0.03-0.05 mrad). The night's ribs did little because they were short, unconnected segments read off end faces, while the tilt optimisation had put its material on the lobes' rims, side walls and waists.
5. **The designs that can still reach the goal are the trade's own forms, built parametrically so the loop can grade them** (section 5): the wheel (spokes from each boss to a rim), the delta (ribs boss-to-boss along the lines of centres, and mount-to-boss), the dish (a conical end wall, thrust carried as membrane), longitudinal gussets along the lobes to the flange lip, waist webs between lobes, and a lattice. Their looks come from the same places as their stiffness: visible load paths, repetition, rims.
6. **The one industrial deck optimises the housing for what the gears feel, with the system model in the loop.** Hexagon and GRM (OED2023) couple the transmission model (shafts, bearings, housing deflection → gear mesh misalignment → edge loading) to a topology optimiser, updating the system solution every few optimisation cycles, and ask for less mass with the misalignment held: 13.5 kg baseline, 7.6 kg optimised, with the same lead correction as the baseline, where a stripped 6.4 kg housing needed a flipped, doubled one. That is our grade's measure and our chain's shape, stated by the trade: "The gear microgeometry is used to refine the design and not for correcting a poor detailed design."
7. **What the agent decides, in this trade:** which measure binds and where (the diagnosis), which family of structure answers it, the design space to give an optimiser, how to read its result into the vocabulary, and when a sizing sweep beats a second optimisation (Ivanov's own advice).

Three things the table makes plain:
- **The studies start one step ahead of us.** Their baseline is a designed housing and the optimiser is a corrector. Our baseline is a shell with no structure, and we asked the optimiser to invent the structure in a voxel model four to eight times too stiff. The trade's answer is to give the shell its structure first, from the vocabulary, and let the optimiser and the sizing sweep correct it.
- **The design space is drawn from the diagnosis.** Son 2020 faults earlier work whose design areas were chosen "based on experience or without clear criteria" and draws its own from the radiated power's peaks. Our diagnosis now exists (section 4).
- **Manufacturing rules enter at step 6, not after.** Ivanov's runs without member sizes and a pull direction "thicken the plates" and make shapes that "cannot be split-molded" (p. 60). Our voxel runs had neither.

## 3. The art: what the designers put where, and why

### 3.1 The AVL housing itself (Ivanov Figs 20-21, 36; the two CAD views) *(figure)*

- **Three parts:** a motor-side housing, a back-side cover, and a separate flange plate (a ring with eight motor holes and ears, bolted to the housing by six bolts, Fig. 20). The flange plate carries the motor and, in the thesis's model, the whole reaction torque into the housing through its bolt areas (Fig. 36: the torque at the shaft centre, coupled to the bolt areas as radial lines). Our lobed form has the plate too (the motor flange plate on the input's pod).
- **The motor face is a wheel.** Behind the round motor face, ribs radiate from the input bearing's boss to the flange's ring (Fig. 20, the lower-right view; the CAD view's thick ring with six holes). The hub is the boss, the spokes are ribs, the rim is the flange's bolted ring. A point load or a moment at the hub goes into the rim as spoke tension and compression, not as plate bending. This is the form our input pod's end wall lacks.
- **The cover is triangulated.** On the back side (Fig. 20, upper left and lower left; Fig. 21), ribs run from the output bore radially to the flange's bolt ears, a diamond of ribs closes round the intermediate bore, and one long rib runs from a top mount ear diagonally to the output boss. Every rib ends on a boss, an ear or another rib: no rib ends in the open. The flange ears along the parting line are gusseted to the wall underneath (the side view).
- **Frame mounts on the top**, two on the motor side and a rear support on the back side, each a boss with a gusset into the wall; the original has a rib between the two top mounts, "probably ... made after sophisticated modeling, empirical load definitions and by skilled designers" (p. 59), which no optimisation run reproduced. Oil inlet and outlet on the back side, a pump outlet at the top (Fig. 21), each a boss bridged into the wall.
- **A bearing cap** takes the output gear's axial force "upon the housing structure" (p. 36): the thrust enters through a bolted cap, not through the wall's own shoulder.
- **Walls between the ribs are thin**; the mass sits in the ribs, ears, bosses and rims. The stripped housing (ribs removed, −749 g, about a tenth of the mass) deflects up to three times more at the bearing centres (Fig. 59 against Fig. 51).

### 3.2 What the optimiser proposed on that housing, and how it was read (Figs 38, 60-71)

- **The chunk** (Fig. 38): a solid block with the bores and mount holes, the gear cavity cut out, meshed at 278,981 TET10 *(figure; the count is in the image, not the text)*. Its result (Fig. 60) "can only provide a rather rough guidance" (p. 59). It is "FEM brainstorming", "unneeded when context designs are already made" (p. 46).
- **The design-space blocks** (Figs 42, 65-67) are the voids between the housing's features, conformal to the shells: the optimiser works on the surfaces of the designed housing, in the room its features leave. With member sizes 7-15 mm and a pull direction along the shafts per block, the results (Fig. 66) are rib-like patches on the end faces: round the output bore on the back side, between the bore and the flange ears, and on the motor side between the top mounts and the bearing bosses. The run that minimised the six bearing-centre displacements (Fig. 67) put material in the same places, in larger patches round the bores *(figure)*.
- **The reading** (Fig. 68) is done across runs for what recurs: "A rib between the fixing ear and bearing hold is suggested in every simulation"; "in all the runs the solver tries 'to bridge' the oil outlet port"; a bridge "between the rear back side support and a connection boss"; and what is absent: nothing on the rear flat face of the motor part, and never the top-mount link that the designers had built (p. 62).
- **The designer's drawing** (Fig. 69): the blue patches become straight ribs, drawn as bars from boss to ear and boss to boss over the red housing, on both parts *(figure)*. Then the shapes are exported from Abaqus into CATIA beside the CAD (Fig. 70) and merged by hand into a proposal (Fig. 71): tall ribs from the top ears diagonally to the output boss, a frame of ribs round the intermediate bore, radial ribs from the output bore to the flange ears, ribs along the flange between its ears, and spokes under the motor face *(figure)*. "Most of the design suggestions were respected, except the ones that disturb the functionality of the gearbox features" (p. 64).
- **The verdict:** at a mass 57 g from the original, the proposal deflected more at the output bearing (0.052 → 0.066 mm forward, 0.138 → 0.163 mm backward, Fig. 76) and was discarded, with the advice to grade the raw result before blaming the reading (p. 66), and to run a second cycle on design spaces redrawn round the first result, or a sizing sweep where the shape is obvious (pp. 63, 68).

### 3.3 The agricultural EV gearbox, three studies on one housing (Son 2019, Son 2020, Kim 2024)

*(figure, with the earlier note's facts; the subagents' details below when they return)*

- **The housing** (Son 2020 Fig. 11; Kim Fig. 12) *(subagent, from the rendered figures)*: two aluminium castings, 4.84 kg in all: an upper case whose face is a round dish and which carries a long cylindrical boss for a bearing, and a lower case with an oval pocket and a ribbed bore; flange ears along the split. Two-stage helical, 7 kW at 2,650 rpm, ratio 12.37, the first stage's mesh frequency 618 Hz.
- **Son 2019: find the softest part, then gusset its root, sized by stiffness per gram.** The model is updated to a free-free modal test (six modes, 1,353-2,939 Hz) within 2.84 %. Local stiffness is read at 65 surface points by driving-point accelerance (Fig. 8), the points grouped by hand into six parts, each part's stiffness taken as ω²/k at the dominant peak. The cylindrical boss is 5-11 times softer than the other five parts (2.8 × 10² against 1.5-3.2 × 10³ N/mm). *Where* on it: the first mode's elastic strain (Fig. 10, 1,400 Hz) peaks on the wall at the boss's root, so that is where the ribs go. The rib is one form, a right-triangular gusset between the boss and the wall (the rib figure: W its thickness, L its length along the wall, H its height up the boss), copied from a 4 mm gusset the housing already had. Two studies: at a constant rib mass of 15.2 g, W 4/6/8 mm gives +34.5/+28.4/+24.6 %, so thin and long beats thick; then at W = 4, L 10-20 by H 15-35 mm gives +11.5 to +57.8 %, scored as percent stiffness per gram (1.5-2.3 %/g). Chosen W 4, L 20, H 25 mm: the best per gram, +43.8 % for 19 g. The modification model (Fig. 12) rings the boss's root with about eight such gussets: +43.3 % local stiffness, the first mode 1,400 → 1,519 Hz, −1.87 dB peak accelerance, +19 g on 4.84 kg. FE only; the casting was not modified.
- **Son 2020: find the weak panels by what radiates, then optimise only there.** The load is the first stage's mesh harmonics that sit within 10 dB(A) of the measured 64-82 dB(A) overall; the internals are Craig-Bampton substructures (RomaxNEXUS, OptiStruct) tied to the seats by rigid elements, so the bearing forces change with the housing; both rotation directions. Three steps name the weak areas: equivalent radiated power over speed peaks at 3,500 and 2,636 rpm clockwise (3,788 and 2,724 counter-clockwise), matched to modes at 813, 1,238, 885 and 1,280 Hz; the operating deflection shapes at those speeds are largest at the boss's end; the element strain energy of the mode behind each shape marks the panels: the boss's root on the upper case, the lower case's wall beside the pocket and between the pocket and the bore, and the bottom flange. A design layer is drawn by hand over those walls (Fig. 16, red: the upper case's lower wall and the boss's outer surface, the lower case's central wall); topology optimisation for least radiated power at a mass cap of +20 %, no manufacturing rule; elements of density ≥ 0.8 kept: collars and webs round the boss's base, arcs along the pocket's flanks, a ring round the bore (Fig. 17). Gain: +12 % mass (4.84 → 5.44 kg); ERP at 2,650 rpm −9.7/−4.2 dB clockwise, −2.7/−5.7 counter-clockwise, worse at some other speeds; the result "has not been laid out to a level that can be actually manufactured", and no prototype. The paper faults earlier design areas chosen "based on experience or without clear criteria".
- **Kim 2024: three design domains, one of which did nothing** (Fig. 12) *(subagent; the paper calls the halves LH and RH, not housing and cover)*. The objective is the A-weighted sound pressure at the microphone, 0-700 Hz, from a structural-acoustic model validated on a rig (modes within 6 %, the run-up within 0.2 dB(A)); the transmission is a Craig-Bampton superelement tied by rigid elements from each bearing's centre node to its seat. The domains are slabs of material added to the housing with shared nodes, chosen "considering the assembly of the agricultural UTV body, drive motor, and housing": domain 1 a flat region of the LH outer wall toward the output bore, domain 2 the RH wall wrapping the output bearing's boss down to the rim, domain 3 the RH upper region round the input bore; volume ≤ 30 % of each. Domain 3 "had no significant effect" and was dropped. Domain 1 kept a strip along its outer edge; domain 2 a band hugging the output boss down to the rim. The user sets the threshold; the ribs were then drawn by hand, "designed to consider the manufacturability and assembly": one straight rib on the LH half, two or three from the output boss to the lower rim on the RH half. Gain: −3.46 dB(A) over 1,250-2,500 rpm, −2.43 dB(A) overall, simulated; 4.836 → 4.847 kg. No stress, deformation or stiffness result; the housing was never built.

The thread through the three: **the weak place is measured first, with the measure that matters, and the design happens there.** The structure that results is always the vocabulary's: gussets at a boss root, ribs across a flat panel, ribs from a boss to the rim.

### 3.4 Hexagon and GRM, OED2023: the housing optimised for the gears' misalignment *(subagent; the text layer of the deck; its charts could not be read)*

- **The argument** (slides 4-9): "Lightweight structures are inherently more flexible"; the system's deflection under load (shaft, bearing, housing) misaligns the gear mesh, which loads the tooth edges and leads to micro-pitting; lead crowning and lead slope corrections cure a small, steady misalignment but not a large one that varies across operating points; and excessive corrections bring flank twist, cost and "relatively poor gear performance".
- **The workflow** (slide 10): Romax Enduro's transmission model (shafts, bearings, gears, misalignment, transmission error, contact stress) coupled to GRM's topology optimiser, with the loop "Assess current topology stiffness → Update solution in Romax Enduro" every n optimisation cycles: "Casing stiffness vs Gear misalignment". The housing's stiffness is re-solved into the system model as the topology changes, so the misalignment the optimiser sees is the gears', not a proxy.
- **The case** (slide 11): a two-stage single-speed EDU, ratio 9.1, 160 N·m maximum input torque, drive and coast at maximum torque; "Optimise the EDU housing to reduce gear mesh misalignment, and reduce mass whilst keeping the stresses within material yield". The design space, the misalignment limit and the exact objective are not on the slides.
- **The three designs** (slides 12-16): the baseline, 13.5 kg; a minimum-mass model, the baseline thinned and defeatured, 6.4 kg; the optimised model, GRM's topology interpreted by hand, 7.6 kg. The interpretation rules (slide 13): "predominantly 5mm thick ribs"; "Mounting features extended and reinforced with ribs"; "Bulk reinforcement material translated to a cross-hatched lattice of ribs"; "Ribs extending outwards to circumference in radial fashion". The verdict is the gears': the optimised housing runs with the baseline's lead slope (−20 µm), the stripped one needs +45 µm, opposite in sign and more than twice the size.
- **Its conclusions** (slide 17): lightweighting "without compromising on the gear performance"; a better contact distribution and TE "without the need to apply large amount of microgeometry corrections"; "The gear microgeometry is used to refine the design and not for correcting a poor detailed design"; the approach exposes "the interaction between strength and stiffness", "too complex to do 'blind'", and "allowing the blending of manual iteration with the understanding of complex system interactions".

For us this deck is the trade saying our plan's shape: the housing is graded by what the gears feel (our seat tilt against the shaft is the bearing's part of it; the mesh misalignment between two shafts is the next measure to add to the judge), the loads come from a system model (our load tool), the optimiser's output is read by hand into 5 mm ribs (a lattice, radial ribs to the rim, reinforced mounts), and the hand-read design is the one that counts. A thesis on the same subject, "Gearbox housing topology optimization with respect to gear misalignment" (Linköping, DiVA diva2:587479), surfaced in the search and is not yet read.

### 3.5 The vocabulary, with its mechanics

| Move | Seen in | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Hub, spokes, rim** | AVL's motor face; Son 2019's radial gussets; our old fan ribs, partly | A moment or an axial force at the hub goes into the rim as in-plane forces in the spokes. The end wall stops being a plate in bending. Needs a rim to end on: the flange ring, the flange lip, or a ring cast on the end face |
| **Ear to boss** | "in every simulation" (Ivanov p. 62); the original's diagonal rib | The straight load path from the bearing to the ground. The mount reaction and the bearing force meet along it |
| **Boss to boss, along the line of centres** | The original's diamond and radial ribs; Fig. 71 | The gear mesh's separating force acts along the line of centres and its tangential force across it; a rib between the bosses carries the first directly and stiffens the wall against the second |
| **Triangles, never open quadrilaterals** | Every ribbed face in the figures | A triangle of ribs is a truss; a quadrilateral is a mechanism until its wall carries shear |
| **Gussets at a boss root and under an ear** | Son 2019's H/W/L rib, a ring of eight at the boss's root; AVL's flange ears | The boss and the ear stop bending at their root; the wall takes the load as shear. Thin and long or tall beats thick: a rib's stiffness grows with the cube of its depth and only linearly with its thickness, so at equal mass the 4 mm gusset gained a third more than the 8 mm one. "The stiffener belongs between the bearing and the housing fixing" (Inoue, as Son 2019 cites it) |
| **A continuous rim** | The flange lip; the motor ring; Fig. 71's ribs along the flange | Ribs need something stiff to end on; a rim closes the load paths round the face |
| **Bridges over ports** | "the solver tries to bridge the oil outlet port" (p. 62) | A port cuts the wall's load path; a bridge restores it |
| **A lattice where the optimiser wants bulk** | Hexagon slide 13: "bulk reinforcement material translated to a cross-hatched lattice of ribs" | A field of solid density on a wall means the wall wants bending stiffness there; crossed ribs give it at a fraction of the bulk's mass |
| **Mounts extended and reinforced** | Hexagon slide 13; AVL's gusseted mounts and top-mount rib | The mount is where every load leaves the housing; its boss is extended into the wall and braced |
| **Thin walls between ribs** | All; Ivanov's stripped housing shows the ribs are a tenth of the mass and most of the stiffness | Mass in the ribs, where the bending stiffness grows with the cube of the depth; the wall only closes the housing |

## 4. What our housing lacks, measured

This morning's diagnostic (`prototypes/first_loop/endwall.py`, on the stored 8 mm answer of `lobed_e10`, no solve; the picture `data/first_loop/endwall_lobed_e10.png`) splits what each bearing feels, its seat's tilt against its shaft's line, into three parts:

| Part | Drive | Reverse | Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| The end wall's slope at the seat (the seat turns with its wall) | 0.18-1.03 mrad; 75-95 % of each seat's own rotation | 0.36-0.74 mrad | The largest |
| The seat's own rotation beyond its wall (the boss and seat bending) | 0.02-0.20 mrad | 0.01-0.34 mrad (0.34 at the intermediate −z under the 12.2 kN thrust) | Small |
| The shaft's line tilting because the lobes bend sideways (the two seats shift 25-50 µm relative to each other) | 0.36-0.50 mrad | 0.24-0.58 mrad | As large as the wall's slope at the output and input |
| The two halves turning against each other at the split | 0.05 mrad, 3 µm shift | 0.03 mrad | Nothing |

In drive the cover's end faces bow outward, most at the intermediate's pod (its +z seat takes the 11.7 kN thrust) and over the output's lobe; the motor side's output lobe is pulled in round its bore. In reverse the output's +z end wall bulges 150 µm and the intermediate's −z pod is pulled 100 µm in. The convex shell tells the same story with larger numbers (walls 0.7-3.4 mrad, line tilts 0.5-2.1 mrad, the joint 0.03-0.04).

So the housing is weak in exactly the two places the trade's vocabulary treats:
- **its end walls are plates in bending**, with no hub-spoke-rim, no boss-to-boss ribs and no ear-to-boss ribs; 10 mm of flat wall bought 43 % for 0.83 kg, and the night's ribs, short segments read off the end faces, bought 10-15 % for 0.4-1 kg;
- **its lobes are thin tubes with nothing along them**: no gussets from the flange lip to the end wall, no webs at the waists, so the lobes bend sideways under the radial seat loads. The tilt optimisation put its material on the lobes' rims, side walls and waists (`topo_lobed_tilt_1kg_6mm.png`), and the reader could not read them.

The joint is not the problem, and the bosses are not the problem; the shells are. That is the diagnosis the trade asks for before drawing a design space.

**The targets, against the shaft** (the lines are 0.75 × the contract's limits): the intermediate's seats must come from 1.26 to 0.375 mrad (3.4×), the output's from 1.62 to 0.75 (2.2×), the input's +z from 1.25 to 0.6 (2.1×). The shaft's own bending slope at the intermediate's bearings uses 0.13-0.15 mrad of the 0.5 mrad limit; if the grade adds it as proposed on the loop's page, the housing's share there is nearer 0.23 mrad, a 5.5× reduction. Ribs that gain tens of percent will not do it; the structure must change in kind.

## 5. Designs that can still reach the goal

Each family below is a form the shell tool can build from parameters, so the loop can grade it like any other design. The mechanics says which of the three parts of section 4 it attacks. Mass estimates assume 6 mm ribs in EN AC-42100 (0.39 g per mm of a 25 mm-deep rib) and are mine, to be replaced by the CAD's mass; the budget on the 6 mm-walled lobed shell (6.66 kg) is about 1.07 kg to the reference's 7.73 kg. The trade's sizing rule holds for all of them: ribs thin and deep, scored by the measure gained per gram (Son 2019's 4 mm gussets; GRM's 5 mm ribs); the process profile's least section, not stiffness, sets the thickness.

1. **The wheel** (hub, spokes, rim on every end face). Spokes from each boss to the pod's rim, 20-30 mm deep, 6 mm thick, every 30-45°, drafted along the shafts; the rim is the flange lip on the split side and, on the end face, a ring cast on the pod's wall or the wall carried 10 mm past the end face. Attacks the wall's slope at the seat, the largest part. The AVL motor face is one. About 25-30 g per spoke; 30-40 spokes over the six end faces: 0.8-1.0 kg. **First to try.** Its look: a machine's face, like a wheel's.
2. **The delta and the ears.** Ribs boss-to-boss along the lines of centres (input-intermediate 83 mm, intermediate-output 125 mm, input-output about 150 mm) on each end face, and a rib from each mount to its nearest boss. Attacks the wall's slope and the lobes' bending together (the ribs tie the three lobes' end walls into one plate). About 0.3 kg for the deltas, 0.1 kg for the ears. Combined with the wheel it is Fig. 71.
3. **The dish.** The end wall of a pod as a shallow cone from the boss collar to the pod's wall, the apex at the boss, rising 15-25 mm outward (within the packaging margin A3). The thrust then goes into the cone as membrane force, not plate bending; the cone adds less than a tenth of the wall's mass. Attacks the wall's slope under the thrusts (the intermediate's seats, the output's +z). Castable in a printed mould; distinctive, like a bell housing. Its weakness: a cone is soft against a moment at its apex, so it wants spokes too.
4. **Longitudinal gussets.** Ribs along each lobe from the flange lip to the end wall's rim, every 45°, 6 mm thick, 15-20 mm deep. Attacks the lobes' sideways bending (the line tilt) and stiffens the end wall's edge. About 22 g each, 12 per half: 0.5 kg for both halves. The AVL housing has them under its flange ears.
5. **Waist webs.** Webs in the re-entrant corners where two pods meet, from the flange lip to the end faces, 6-8 mm thick. They make the three lobes bend as one body. Where the tilt optimisation put much of its material. About 0.2 kg.
6. **Thick where it counts.** End walls 6 mm everywhere except a collar 14-16 mm thick to 1.6 × the seat's diameter round each boss, blended. Cheap mass, modest gain; a complement to 1-5, not an alternative (10 mm everywhere bought 43 %).
7. **The lattice.** A cross-hatched or triangular grid of 4-5 mm ribs, 15-20 mm deep, over each end face, ending on the rim and the bosses: what GRM made of the optimiser's bulk on the EDU housing (5 mm ribs, 13.5 → 7.6 kg at the baseline's misalignment). The stiffest plate per kilogram, modern to look at, castable in printed sand. Its cost is the reader and the builder: many short ribs with drafts and fillets. Second round, if the wheel falls short.
8. **The optimiser's own ribs, read everywhere.** The tilt optimisation re-run on the designed shell (family 1 + 4 as the frozen base), with member sizes and a pull direction per half, in a local design space over the binding seats, and its result read on all faces (end faces, side walls, waists), not the end faces only. The trade's step 10.

**What to run first, in order:** the wheel on both halves at 6 mm end walls (1); then wheel + gussets (1 + 4); then wheel + delta + ears (1 + 2); then the dish under the wheel at the intermediate's pods (3); each graded against `lobed_e10` on 8 mm meshes, the best confirmed at 6 mm; then a sizing sweep of the winner (spoke depth 20/25/30, count, gusset depth), as Son 2019 and Ivanov advise. Twelve to fifteen judgements. The single-precision fix in `fe.py` comes before any of them (the loop's page, Open).

**What makes these interesting rather than merely stiff.** The forms the trade admires show their load paths: a wheel says where the torque goes, a delta says where the gears push, a dish says where the thrust goes. Repetition (spokes at equal angles), symmetry about each shaft, ribs that end on rims and bosses, equal rib depths, fillets at every root and a draft on every face make a housing look designed; ribs that stop in the open, unequal depths and patches do not. An agent that composes from the vocabulary and refuses the rest will produce housings that look like the AVL original's family, and a lattice or a dish will make ones no one has cast yet.

## 6. What the tools and the agent take from this

- **The shell tool gets a vocabulary, not a reader's output:** spokes (count, depth, thickness, draft) to a rim; a rim (depth) on each end face; boss-to-boss and mount-to-boss ribs; gussets along the lobes; waist webs; a conical end wall; a collar. Every piece parametric, rooted on named faces, filleted, drafted, checked against the gears' and tools' room.
- **The diagnosis is a tool** (`endwall.py` today; the product's judge later): the wall's share, the seat's share, the lobes' share, the joint's share, per seat and case, with the picture. The design space and the family are chosen from it, as Son 2020 chooses its panels from the radiated power.
- **The judge gains the gears' measure.** Beside each seat's tilt against its shaft (the bearing's misalignment), the mesh misalignment of each gear pair: the change in the angle between the two shafts' lines in the plane of action, from the four seats' motions and the shafts' own slopes. It is what Hexagon's loop holds and what the lead correction absorbs; a limit for it belongs in the contract, with its source.
- **The optimiser works on a designed shell, locally, with casting rules** (member sizes, a pull direction per half), and its reader reads every face.
- **The sizing sweep is a tool:** a family's few parameters swept over 6-12 proofs, the lightest pass kept.
- **The agent** names the binding measure and its cause from the diagnosis, picks the family and its first sizes, decides the design space and the objective for the optimiser, reads its result into the vocabulary, drops what breaks a function and says why, and chooses between a second optimisation and a sweep. Every choice is recorded with its reason and graded by the tools.
