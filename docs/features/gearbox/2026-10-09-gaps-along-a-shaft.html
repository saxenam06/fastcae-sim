Gaps along a shaft

Step 5 of the calculation chain places each gear and bearing along its shaft from three gaps you give. This page shows what those gaps hold, what they change, and why the smallest is not always the best. Move the sliders.

1

What fills the gap between a bearing and its gear

The gap is not empty space. Three things must fit in it, side by side. On the shaft: the shoulder the bearing's inner ring rests on, and the retaining ring that holds the gear. At the housing: the lip that locates the bearing's outer ring, plus a clearance so the turning gear never touches it. And room for a puller's jaws to grip the bearing when it is taken off. The gap must be at least the largest of these.

2

A gear between its bearings: the gap sets the span

The span is the distance between the two bearings' centres: the bearing width, two gaps and the gear's face width. The shaft is a beam on two supports, and the gear's force bends it. Bending grows with the cube of the span, so a few millimetres of gap make a real difference to how much the gear moves and tilts.

For a gear between its bearings, a smaller gap is a shorter span: a stiffer shaft, less sag, better gear alignment. The axial force also tips the shaft through its lever (3,243 N × 27.3 mm), loading one bearing more than the other; a shorter span makes that difference larger.

3

An overhung gear: here a short span is worse

Some gears sit outside their two bearings, at the end of a shaft. Then the bearings act like a lever and its pivot. The bearing next to the gear carries more than the gear's force, the far one is pulled the other way, and the end of the shaft sags. A long span between the bearings, against a short overhang, is what keeps this in check: Łódź asks for a span of at least three times the overhang. Ivanov's gears all sit between their bearings, so for that layout short spans are the good case.

4

The seat needs material around it

A bearing is held only by the housing wall around its bore. Under the shaft's load a thin wall bends, the bearing tilts with it, and a tilted bearing puts the gears out of line. That misalignment is exactly what the housing is judged on. Thickness helps a lot, because a wall's stiffness against bending grows with the cube of its thickness; ribs behind the seat help more.

Seat tilt, relative to an 8 mm wall

A relative picture, for intuition: the tilt scales as one over the thickness cubed, and the ribs are counted as tripling the stiffness. The FE judge computes the real tilt, against the contract's limits: 0.5 mrad for a tapered bearing, 0.8 for a cylindrical roller, 1.0 for a ball bearing (Shigley).

5

Oil and heat: gears need room in the oil

The gears dip into the oil at the bottom of the housing and drag it round. Packed tight against the walls, a gear traps the oil and shears it; that drag is lost power, and it turns into heat. With room around the gear the oil falls back freely. So the radial gap from the gear's tips to the wall has a lower limit too. The Łódź handbook keeps it at 1.1 to 1.2 times the wall's thickness δ.

For Ivanov's 125 mm stage, Łódź's wall is δ = 0.025 × 125 + 1 = 4.1 mm, raised to its 7.5 mm floor for sand casting; so the guide is 8.3 to 9.0 mm.

6

Where the housing puts its material: the trade-off

To stiffen a seat the housing needs more material around the bearing. Material on the outside of the bearing (a boss, ribs on the outer face) stiffens the seat without moving it, if the package has room there. Material on the inside, between the gear and the bearing, pushes the bearing outward: the seat is stiffer, but the span grows and the shaft bends more. With a tight package the only room is inside, and then there is a balance to find. This is the choice zenryx's agent could explore.

7

The round: steps 5 to 8 go round until they agree

The gaps stay fixed; what changes from pass to pass is the bearings' width. The layout needs the widths, the loads need the layout, the shaft needs the loads, and the bearings need the loads and the shaft. So the tool starts from a guess and goes round. Step through one example.

8

Three shafts: a gap on one shaft keeps gears on another apart

Look along the shafts. Ivanov's output wheel is big: its outer edge reaches 99 mm from the output shaft, which is only 125 mm from the intermediate shaft. So it comes within 26 mm of the intermediate shaft's centre, while the stage-1 wheel on the intermediate shaft reaches 57 mm out. In this view the two wheels cover the same ground. They do not collide only because they sit at different places along the shafts.

Now look from the side, each shaft drawn as its own row. The output wheel sits beside the stage-2 pinion it meshes with. Between it and the stage-1 wheel there is only the gap between the two gears on the intermediate shaft. That gap's size is not set by the radii: the radii only tell that the gap must also keep two turning gears of different shafts apart.

Calculating the clearances

This is the check the tool would run by itself once step 5 has placed everything. First it finds every pair of parts on different shafts that overlap in the end view. Then, for each, it measures their distance along the shafts and compares it with the smallest clearance allowed. A pair that falls short fails the layout, by name. Pairs that are apart in the end view need no check.

so

Is the smallest gap always best?

  • For a gear between its bearings, mostly yes: a shorter span is a stiffer shaft, so less sag and tilt at the gear, and a narrower, lighter housing (2).
  • But never below what must fit in it: the shoulder, the ring, the lip and its clearance, the puller's room (1).
  • Not for an overhung gear, which wants a long span (3).
  • Not at the cost of the seat: it needs its material (4), and material inside the wall lengthens the span (6).
  • And the gears need room in the oil (5).
  • With several shafts, a gap on one shaft can be what keeps gears on another apart: the tool checks every pair that overlaps when seen along the shafts (8).
